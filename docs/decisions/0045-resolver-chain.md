# 0045 Resolver chain of the Android filter

## Context

The Android filter forwarded every allowed lookup to Quad9 alone ([0033](0033-android-filter-network-access.md)). On 22 October 2026, Quad9 announced that from 24 November 2026 it will no longer serve users located in France, after a French court ruling in a case brought by beIN. It has not said how it will cut them off: a dropped connection, a refused one, a TLS failure, a `REFUSED` or `SERVFAIL` answer to everything are all possible. With one resolver, the filter would stop resolving anything there.

A plain fallback to the system's resolver would send the names in clear and let the network answer around the filter. Trying a failed resolver before each query would add its timeout to every lookup.

## Decision

- The filter forwards to an ordered chain of three resolvers, each over DNS over TLS on port 853, at hard-coded addresses, with the certificate checked for its name against the certificates that ship with Android only, TLS 1.2 and later:
  1. Quad9, `dns.quad9.net`: 9.9.9.9, 149.112.112.112, 2620:fe::fe, 2620:fe::9.
  2. Cloudflare's malware-blocking resolver, `security.cloudflare-dns.com`: 1.1.1.2, 1.0.0.2, 2606:4700:4700::1112, 2606:4700:4700::1002.
  3. DNS4EU Protective, `protective.joindns4.eu`: 86.54.11.1, 86.54.11.201, 2a13:1001::86:54:11:1, 2a13:1001::86:54:11:201.

  The addresses and names were checked against each operator's documentation on 2026-11-20. All three block malware, so the protection does not change with the resolver. All their addresses are already among the public resolvers that the tunnel routes ([0038](0038-content-filter.md)), and their names are in the strict mode list, which a test checks.

- Cloudflare comes before DNS4EU for the person's privacy. Cloudflare deletes its resolver logs and the truncated client addresses within 25 hours, and says a Big Four accounting firm audits these practices and publishes a report; its page does not say whether the audit covers the malware-blocking resolver. DNS4EU keeps query logs, with a pseudonym of the address, for up to six months, and shares them with its project partners. Both block malware, so the order changes what is kept, not the protection. Cloudflare is a US company, DNS4EU an EU project run from the Czech Republic: the order puts the shorter retention before the jurisdiction.
- On 17 September 2026, a Paris court ruled that Cloudflare does not have to block sites on 1.1.1.1; it blocks them on its CDN instead. Canal+ can appeal, so Cloudflare's place in France is not settled either, which is why DNS4EU stays third rather than being dropped.

- A query has short budgets for each step, with every resolver, kept connections included: 1 second to connect, across the resolver's addresses, 2 seconds for the TLS handshake and 3 seconds for the answer. A resolver fails a query on a timeout of these budgets, a refused or reset connection, a TLS or certificate failure, any other error, unchecked exceptions of the TLS stack included, or a `REFUSED` answer. The query then goes to the next resolver. A kept connection that times out is not tried again over a new one.
- `SERVFAIL`, `NXDOMAIN` and every other answer are final and go back to the app. A `SERVFAIL` also starts a probe of that resolver in the background, so that a resolver that answers everything with errors is found out and leaves the order.
- Each resolver is probed in the background with a query for the root's name servers. A probe passes only on `NOERROR` with at least one `NS` record; `SERVFAIL`, `NXDOMAIN`, `REFUSED`, an empty answer or any error fail it. Probes have longer budgets: 12 seconds to connect across the addresses, at most 3 seconds each, 5 seconds for the handshake and 5 for the answer.
- The chain is probed when the tunnel starts and on every change of the phone's networks, seen through a network callback that compares the set of networks, so that the networks present at the start do not count as a change. A network change makes every resolver not probed yet: what passed or failed on the previous network says nothing of the new one. A probe that started on the previous network does not count, and no probe starts for a resolver while one runs for it on the same network, so a start probes the chain once.
- A resolver that fails a query is marked failing and probed again at once in the background. A failed probe is followed by another after 30 seconds, 2 minutes and 10 minutes, then every hour; a network change starts the waits over. A resolver that passes a probe takes its place in the order again.
- A query goes to the first resolver that passed, then to the others that passed, then to those not probed yet, in order. A resolver known to fail is never tried by a query.
- When every resolver failed, the query gets `SERVFAIL` at once, and the chain is probed again in the background, at most every 30 seconds. Otherwise an outage longer than the first waits would leave the filter without DNS until the hourly probe.
- The connections to the resolvers are kept open and reused, as before ([0033](0033-android-filter-network-access.md)). Stopping the filter closes every socket of the chain at once, kept, in use or still connecting.
- There is no fallback to plain DNS or to the system's resolver. The sockets stay protected, the probes run off the main thread, and no query is logged.
- The code stays where [0033](0033-android-filter-network-access.md) put it: the endpoints in `Resolvers.kt`, formerly `Quad9.kt`, the sockets and the budgets in `ResolverTransport.kt`, the chain in `DotClient.kt`.

## Consequences

- In France, from 24 November 2026, the allowed lookups go to Cloudflare, and to DNS4EU when Cloudflare fails too, whether Quad9 refuses the connection or answers with errors. Elsewhere they still go to Quad9. If Quad9 answered French users with `NXDOMAIN` or wrong addresses to everything but the root's name servers, the chain would not see it: that will be checked from a French network on 24 November.
- A query waits at most 6 seconds on a resolver that stops answering, then moves on, and the queries after it skip that resolver. On a network change, the queries sent before the probes end try the chain in order with these budgets.
- DNS4EU applies court-ordered domain blocks to all its resolvers, Protective included, as its public page says; its policy blocks when an enforceable court decision requires it. Blocks that French courts order, such as those of the beIN ruling that led Quad9 to withdraw, can therefore apply to lookups from France that reach it.
- Three operators can see the allowed lookups, with the phone's IP address, instead of one. What each publishes, read on 2026-11-20:
  - Quad9 does not log client IP addresses.
  - Cloudflare, under its 1.1.1.1 policy, which applies to the malware-blocking resolver: the client's IP address is not written to disk, except in packets sampled from at most 0.05% of the traffic, for troubleshooting and attack mitigation, with no stated retention. The resolver logs hold the name, the type, the answer code, the network's AS number and country, and the address truncated to its first three IPv4 bytes or its first 48 IPv6 bits; they are deleted within 25 hours, except a sample without addresses used for aggregates. The aggregates, among them the list of every name asked with its count and the time it was first asked in each region, are kept without limit. APNIC can query the anonymised logs, names, types and resolver locations but no client address, for non-profit research.
  - DNS4EU, operated by Whalebone in the Czech Republic, under its resolver policy of 2025: the client's IP address stays in memory for the time of the query, and is written to disk only during an attack on the resolver, kept up to six months after it, or in a crash dump, kept up to three months. The query logs, with the name, the answer, the time, the network's AS number and a pseudonym of the address made with a daily key, are kept up to six months for threat research and shared with project partners who train models on them; aggregated data and parts of the anonymised logs may go to threat intelligence partners under written agreements, and aggregates are kept without limit. It does not sell or pass on IP addresses, nor combine the data with other sources, and its public page says queries are not sold, profiled or used for advertising.
- The disclosure before the VPN consent names the three, in order, and no longer says that nothing is recorded.
- A resolver that fails once for a query comes back as soon as a probe passes, usually the one that starts at once. A failure of Quad9 that lasts sends lookups to Cloudflare until a probe passes: within 30 seconds, 2, 10 minutes or an hour, depending on how long it lasts.
- Each tunnel start and each network change costs one TLS connection per resolver, and each `SERVFAIL` at most one probe.
- Strict mode still blocks the three names for every app. flust's own lookups go by address and are not affected.
